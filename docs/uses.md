# What an archive is useful for

## For yourself

- Memory loss: reminiscing, recalling recent events, routines, and a baseline for noticing change early. See [Memory loss](memory-loss.md).
- After a brain injury or stroke. External memory aids show modest benefits for people with moderate to severe brain injury, and an archive can answer "when did I…?" questions.
- Therapy. A timeline of what happened, with what you wrote at the time, gives a therapist more to work with than recollection alone. You can also look for your own patterns: what came before the hard stretches and what helped them end.
- Your health history: diagnoses, lab results, medications and symptoms on one timeline, with the original documents behind each line, ready for a new doctor or an emergency.
- A true record of your work: what you did, when, and with what result, kept apart from the CV. It's the material for performance reviews, job applications and salary talks.
- ADHD and busy lives: a place to look up what you promised, to whom, and when.
- Planning. You can see where you lived, who you were with and what work you did in your good years, and plan from that.

## For papers and proof

- Immigration. Visa petitions ask for evidence: press about you, your publications, your roles, letters, pay. Proof of a relationship or of where you lived needs dated photos, messages and documents. An archive gives you a list of these with dates and sources.
- Legal matters: dates, documents and messages with their original context, instead of screenshots out of order.
- Emigration. A life split across countries, languages and apps is in one place, including the papers each new bureaucracy asks for.

## For family

- A parent's life. Build an archive with a parent while they can tell the stories: their voice, their photos with names, their history.
- After a death, an archive lets a family keep and share a person's words and pictures without logging into accounts they're not allowed to use.
- Something to leave your children: your life in your words, with the photos labeled.
- Family history. Combine relatives' photos and stories (with their agreement) into one family timeline.
- Carers. A "who I am" page tells a carer about the person: preferences, routines, important people, history.

## For making things

- A memoir or a biography. The timeline and what you wrote at the time are the raw material, and they're more reliable than memory.
- A life book: a printed book of photos and posts from each year.
- A digital twin (advanced). A model trained on your own writing sounds like you, and facts drawn from the archive keep it from inventing your life.

## For research

- Donating data to science. Studies of language and aging, mood and health need long personal records. With an archive, you decide what to share, with whom, and for what.

## What it shouldn't be used for

- Watching other people. Your archive holds their words too. Don't publish them, and don't use an archive to monitor anyone.
- Diagnosis. A pattern in your data is a reason to talk to a professional. It isn't an answer.
- Recording everything. You don't have to. Gaps are fine.
