# Can a life archive help with memory loss?

Yes, in a few ways, but it doesn't slow the disease. A life archive can make reminiscing easier, help people recall recent events and get through the day, support families and carers, and give a personal baseline for noticing change early. The evidence for each of these is modest and comes mostly from small studies. This page covers what was known as of late 2026 and what it means for building an archive.

This page summarizes research. It isn't medical advice. Talk to a doctor about any change in memory.

## What the research says

### Reminiscing helps a little, mostly with communication

Reminiscence therapy uses photos, music and objects as prompts to talk about the past. The 2018 Cochrane review (22 trials; 1,749 people in the meta-analysis) found a small benefit to cognition right after treatment that didn't last, a probable slight benefit to communication, inconsistent effects on quality of life, and no harms. One-to-one reminiscence was linked to better cognition and mood. [Woods et al., 2018]

For an archive: it supplies the prompts, as real photos, places, music and words from the person's own life.

### Life story books help relationships with carers

A life story book collects a person's history, people and preferences. Reviews find positive effects on communication and on the relationship between the person and their carers, but the studies are small. Digital versions are acceptable to people with dementia, but when people are left to use an app on their own, engagement is low. A life story book works best when someone uses it with them. [Elfrink et al., 2018; review of reviews, 2023]

### Looking back at pictures of recent events helps recall

In a study with six people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's, reviewing pictures from a small wearable camera led to more details of recent significant events being recalled than reviewing a written diary: in five of six people at two weeks, and four of five at three months. The study was tiny, but the effect was consistent. [Woodberry et al., 2015]

For an archive: dated photos of recent events with their places, reviewed regularly, help more than notes alone.

### A structured calendar and notebook help in mild cognitive impairment

In a trial with 40 people with amnestic mild cognitive impairment, people trained to use a calendar-and-notebook system managed daily life better and felt more confident about their memory, and their care partners' mood improved. The benefit needed training and faded without practice. [Greenaway et al., 2013]

For an archive: keep routines, upcoming events and "what happened this week" in one simple place that is used daily, with help at the start.

### Changes in language can show up years early

- In the Nun Study, nuns whose autobiographies, written in their early twenties, had low "idea density" (few ideas per word) were much more likely to develop Alzheimer's six decades later. [Snowdon et al., 1996]
- In the Framingham Heart Study, short written picture descriptions from people with normal cognition predicted who would develop Alzheimer's, on average 7.6 years later, with an accuracy of about 70%. [Eyigoz et al., 2020]

For an archive: years of your own messages, posts and notes are a baseline, and a change measured against your own past is more informative than a comparison with strangers. These measures are research tools and don't diagnose anything. A change is a reason to see a doctor.

### Noticing early matters more than it used to

- The first blood test for Alzheimer's pathology (Fujirebio's Lumipulse pTau217/β-amyloid 1-42 ratio) was cleared by the FDA in May 2025, for adults with symptoms, as part of a specialist's evaluation. [FDA clearance, 2025]
- The treatments approved for Alzheimer's (lecanemab, donanemab) are for the early stages only: mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia. Their benefit is modest and they carry risks, which are higher for carriers of the APOE ε4 gene.
- A lifestyle program helps. In the U.S. POINTER trial (2,111 sedentary adults aged 60–79), a structured program of exercise, diet, cognitive and social activity and health checks improved cognition over two years, a little more than a self-guided one, with benefits regardless of APOE ε4 status. [U.S. POINTER, JAMA 2025]

For an archive: knowing when changes started, with dates and examples, helps a doctor. Records of sleep, activity and social time might support a lifestyle program, but that part isn't proven.

### What didn't work

- Personalized music playlists didn't reduce agitation in a trial across 54 nursing homes. Music may still bring joy and connection, but it shouldn't be expected to treat symptoms. [McCreedy et al., 2022]
- Apps that people are expected to use alone, without anyone helping, mostly go unused.

## What this means for building an archive

- Start early, while the person can still tell the stories and correct them.
- Choose the contents for the person: names on faces, the places they lived, the ten most important people and how they know them, their work, the music they love, daily routines, what comforts them and what upsets them.
- Keep painful material out of the views meant for reminiscing. Reminiscing can bring up painful memories.
- Keep it simple: photos first, large text, few buttons. In later stages a printed life story book may work better than a screen.
- Use it together. Reminiscence and life story work help most with family or a carer alongside.
- For a baseline, write a short weekly note in your own words.
- Put the paperwork in the archive while the person can still decide it themselves: a power of attorney, an advance directive and medical records.
- Make a "who I am" page for carers: preferences, routines, important people and history.

## What it can't do

- It doesn't slow, stop or reverse Alzheimer's.
- It doesn't diagnose anything.
- Most of the evidence is from small studies, and the benefits are mainly to mood, communication and connection, not to memory itself.
